In the summer of 2025, a research group at the MIT Media Lab published a report that landed in every boardroom. [The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025](https://mlq.ai/media/quarterly_decks/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf), from MIT's Project NANDA, concluded that 95% of organisations were getting zero measurable return from their generative AI investments. Only 5% of integrated pilots were extracting real value.

The models worked. The demos impressed. The pilots died somewhere between the proof of concept and Monday morning.

For boutique firms thinking about AI, this is the most useful finding of the past few years. It tells you exactly what to watch for, and what to ask any partner before you sign.

## Why pilots stall

The NANDA researchers were clear that the divide is not driven by model quality or regulation. It is driven by approach. Of the organisations that evaluated enterprise-grade AI systems, 60% looked at them, 20% reached a pilot, and just 5% reached production. The report names the causes plainly: brittle workflows, a lack of contextual learning, and misalignment with day-to-day operations.

They are not alone. In 2024 [Gartner predicted](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2024-07-29-gartner-predicts-30-percent-of-generative-ai-projects-will-be-abandoned-after-proof-of-concept-by-end-of-2025) that at least 30% of generative AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, citing poor data quality, inadequate risk controls, escalating costs and unclear business value. And the [McKinsey State of AI 2025 survey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai) found that only about a third of organisations say they are scaling AI across the business. The rest are still experimenting or piloting.

Read those lists again. None of them is a technology problem. They are design problems, ownership problems and measurement problems.

### The three groups that never meet

A typical AI pilot involves three groups of people.

The first group builds it. They know the problem from a briefing document. The second group owns the problem, usually partners or senior consultants, and they move on to the next engagement once the kick-off is done. The third group has to use the system every day, and they never saw the briefing at all.

Between those three groups, the knowledge that makes the difference between a demo and a system quietly disappears. Nobody does anything wrong. The information simply does not travel.

Meanwhile, the people in that third group are often already using AI, just not the official kind. The NANDA researchers found that while only 40% of companies had bought an official LLM subscription, workers from over 90% of the companies they surveyed reported regularly using personal AI tools for work. The appetite is there. The fit is missing.

### Clean data meets real life

The pattern is predictable. The pilot runs in a separate environment with tidy sample data. Production runs in reality: duplicate records, a spreadsheet maintained next to the official system, a colleague who has done the process differently for twenty years and does it well.

The pilot never met that reality, so it does not survive it.

## The pyramid makes it worse

In large consultancies, context leaks at every handover. Partner to project lead, project lead to junior team, junior team to an offshore delivery centre, delivery centre to the implementation vendor. Every step is handled professionally. Every step loses a piece of the why.

At the end stands a system that is technically correct and practically unaware of the firm it was built for.

This is not a criticism of those firms. Their model is designed for scale, and scale is exactly what enterprise clients hire them for. But if your firm has 70 people, you are not buying that scale. You are buying the handovers.

## What the 5% do differently

The NANDA report is useful because it does not stop at the bad news. The organisations crossing the divide share a few habits. They pick narrow, high-value use cases. They integrate deeply into existing workflows. They choose tools that learn and adapt instead of static ones. And in the researchers' sample, external partnerships reached deployment about 67% of the time, compared with about 33% for internal builds.

That last number deserves a careful reading. It does not say "hire anyone". It says that partners who bring a tested method and customise deeply to your workflow tend to get further than teams building from scratch.

### A healthy pilot has three properties

From our own experience inside international organisations, a pilot that survives has three things in common.

**It runs in production from day one.** Not in a sandbox. In the actual mailbox, the actual document system, the actual CRM your team opens every morning.

**It is used by the people who do the work.** Not by the project team, not by an enthusiastic partner on a Friday afternoon. By the consultants and support staff whose week it is meant to change.

**It has a stop criterion.** A number above which it moves forward and below which it is honestly stopped. Pilots without a stop criterion never die. They just slowly fade out, and take the firm's appetite for AI with them.

That sounds strict, and it is. It is also the only way to avoid having, a year from now, a demo nobody remembers the purpose of.

## How a boutique firm gets one system live

The boutique answer is often described as "smaller" or "faster". The real difference is continuity. At The Outlier, the order is always the same: measure first, build inside the tools your team already uses, and stay until the numbers prove it. The person who mapped the friction does not write a handover document. That person builds.

### Step one: measure before you build

Every engagement starts with the question of where time and margin actually leak. In a firm of 50 to 100 people, the answer usually sits in a handful of flows: proposals, client onboarding, file building and status updates. The AI Friction Scan maps the three heaviest ones in two weeks and puts hours and euros next to each.

### Step two: pick one system, not three

Ambition kills more pilots than technology does. Choose the flow with the biggest direct time saving and the lowest risk, often the one that the whole partnership can see. Build that. The rest waits for the next quarter.

### Step three: build where the work already happens

Picture what this looks like for a proposal flow. Instead of a new platform, the system lives in the Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace environment where proposals are already written. It drafts the recurring parts from your own past proposals, in your own phrasing. A partner remains the final approval step. The consultant sees their own words coming back, which is exactly why they trust it.

### Step four: make measurement part of the design

Measure before and after, from the start: time per proposal, every week. Then the question of whether it works needs no opinion, only a number. In an AI Systems Sprint of 8 to 12 weeks, that before-and-after measurement is part of the delivery, along with training for the people who use the system and a maintenance plan.

## Four questions to ask any partner

Before you choose a partner, large or small, ask the same four questions:

1. **Who builds the system?** A name and a role, not a team description.
2. **Which tools does it run in at delivery?** If the answer is a new platform, ask why.
3. **How is adoption measured, and by whom?** Adoption is part of the delivery, not a task for afterwards.
4. **What happens if the result falls short of the promise?** A good partner has an answer ready.

If those four answers are sharp, the chance of a living system is real, with any partner. If a provider cannot answer them directly, that is not a sign of bad intent. It is a sign of the wrong model for your size.

## Judgement stays with the expert

AI is good at gathering and summarising. Its weak point is judgement: which of these opportunities matters for this firm, in this market, with these people? That takes years in the field.

A system built around a senior advisor protects that judgement and scales it. The assistant prepares the list. The senior decides. The reasoning behind the decision stays in the system for next time. That way the system grows with the firm instead of beside it.
