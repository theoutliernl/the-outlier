In almost every partnership there is one person who goes quiet when AI comes up. Often it is the most experienced partner in the room. They are not against progress. They are thinking about the client file that ends up somewhere it should not, the regulator who asks a question nobody can answer, and the advice that went out with an error nobody checked.

That partner is right to ask. And the questions have answers.

This guide covers the four things partners in boutique firms most often worry about: GDPR, the EU AI Act, client confidentiality and accountability. It is practical, not exhaustive. **It is not legal advice.** For decisions specific to your firm, involve your privacy officer or legal counsel. What this guide can do is give the partnership a shared starting point.

## You are not alone in hesitating

Legal uncertainty is one of the main reasons firms hold back. According to [Eurostat](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Use_of_artificial_intelligence_in_enterprises), among EU enterprises that had considered AI but did not use it in 2025, 71% cited a lack of relevant expertise, 53% a lack of clarity about the legal consequences, and 49% concerns about data protection and privacy.

Those concerns are reasonable. But waiting does not make them go away, because your people are probably using AI already. The question is whether they do it inside a framework or outside one.

## GDPR: the rules you already know still apply

The good news first. There is no separate privacy law for AI. The [General Data Protection Regulation](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng) applies to AI the same way it applies to your CRM or your payroll system. Your firm already knows these principles. AI simply puts more pressure on them.

### The principles that matter most

- **Purpose limitation and data minimisation (Article 5).** Only put personal data into an AI tool when it is needed for a defined purpose. A draft proposal rarely needs a client contact's home address or an assignee's medical history.
- **A processor agreement (Article 28).** If an AI provider processes personal data on your behalf, you need a data processing agreement with them, just as you do with any other supplier.
- **A data protection impact assessment (Article 35).** Where processing is likely to result in a high risk to people, for example large-scale processing of sensitive data, a DPIA is required before you start.
- **Automated decisions (Article 22).** People have the right not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing that significantly affect them. For recruitment and HR firms, that line matters a great deal.
- **International transfers (Chapter V).** Know where the provider stores and processes data, and on what legal basis it leaves the EU, if it does.

### The risk hiding in plain sight

The most common privacy problem with AI is not a sophisticated attack. It is a well-meaning employee pasting client information into a free chatbot. The Dutch Data Protection Authority has [warned that this can amount to a data breach](https://www.autoriteitpersoonsgegevens.nl/en/current/caution-use-of-ai-chatbot-may-lead-to-data-breaches), and reported receiving breach notifications from organisations where staff entered personal data of patients or customers into AI chatbots. Its advice is straightforward: make clear agreements with employees about AI use and about which data they may and may not enter.

The answer is not a ban. Bans push usage underground. The answer is an approved tool, a clear rule, and a short explanation of why.

## The EU AI Act: what applies to a firm like yours

The [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng) is the world's first broad AI law. It sounds heavy, and for companies that build high-risk AI systems, it is. Most boutique firms are in a different position: they use AI tools built by others. In the Act's language, that makes you a **deployer**, not a provider. Deployers have fewer obligations, but not none.

### AI literacy

Since February 2025, organisations using AI have been expected to address the AI literacy of the people who work with it. The 2026 Digital Omnibus revised this article: as the [IAPP explains](https://iapp.org/news/a/eu-ai-act-literacy-changes-may-complicate-more-than-simplify-compliance), the duty is now framed as taking measures to support the development of AI literacy among staff, with the Commission and member states providing guidance. In practice, the expectation for a boutique firm is sensible: make sure the people using AI understand what it does well, where it fails, and what they remain responsible for.

### Transparency

Since August 2026, transparency rules under Article 50 apply. If people interact with an AI system, such as a chatbot on your website, they should be told they are dealing with AI unless it is obvious. There are also disclosure rules for certain AI-generated or manipulated content. If you publish AI-assisted material, check whether and how these rules apply to you.

### High-risk uses: relevant for HR and recruitment

The Act lists certain uses as high-risk. One of them is employment: AI used to recruit or select people, to filter applications, to evaluate candidates, or to make decisions about promotion, termination or task allocation. For recruitment and HR firms, that is core territory.

The timing has shifted. Under the Digital Omnibus adopted in 2026, the [European Parliament's summary](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/legislative-train/package-digital-package/file-digital-omnibus-on-ai) confirms that rules for stand-alone high-risk AI systems now apply from 2 December 2027, rather than August 2026. That is breathing room, not an exemption. Deployers of high-risk systems will need, among other things, to use them according to the provider's instructions, assign competent human oversight, and monitor how they perform. A firm that builds those habits now will not have to scramble later.

## Client confidentiality: your contracts come first

GDPR protects personal data. Confidentiality protects everything else your clients trust you with: strategy, deal terms, disputes, salary structures, restructuring plans. Much of that is not personal data at all, and it can be even more sensitive.

Three practical checks help:

1. **Read your engagement letters.** Some clients, especially larger ones, have explicit clauses on subcontractors, data location or the use of AI. Know what you have promised.
2. **Choose business-grade tools.** Use AI services under business terms that state your inputs are not used to train the provider's models, with clear data retention settings. Free consumer versions often do not offer this.
3. **Classify your information.** A simple three-level scheme works: public, internal, client-confidential. Then decide which tools may touch which level. Write it on one page.

For regulated professions, such as lawyers, also check the guidance from your own professional body. Their rules on confidentiality apply regardless of the tool.

## Keeping experts accountable

This is the question that matters most to the quiet partner, and the one with the clearest answer: **AI prepares, the expert decides.**

A client hires your firm for judgement. That does not change because a system drafted the first version. The professional who signs the advice owns it, just as they own work prepared by a junior colleague.

Build that principle into the system itself, not just the policy:

- **A named human approves every client-facing output.** No proposal, report or email leaves without a person's sign-off.
- **Sources are visible.** An assistant that answers from the firm's files should show which file each answer came from, so it can be checked in one click.
- **One owner per system.** Name one partner as the owner of each AI system. Not a committee. A name.
- **Errors are logged and learned from.** When the system gets something wrong, someone records it, and the system or the process is adjusted.

This is not bureaucracy. It is the same quality control a good firm already applies to people, applied to a new kind of colleague.

## A one-page starting point

You do not need a thick policy to begin. A first version fits on a single page:

- **Approved tools:** which AI tools may be used, under which business terms.
- **Data rules:** which categories of information may go into which tool.
- **Responsibility:** the professional who signs off owns the output.
- **Training:** a short session for everyone who uses AI, refreshed once a year.
- **Register:** a simple list of where AI is used in the firm, who owns it, and whether a DPIA was needed.
- **Review:** the partnership looks at the page once a quarter.
