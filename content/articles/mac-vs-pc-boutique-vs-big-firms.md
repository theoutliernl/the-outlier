Between 2006 and 2009, Apple ran one of the most quoted campaigns in advertising history. Two men stood against a white background. One said "I'm a Mac". The other said "I'm a PC". The [Get a Mac campaign](https://www.cultofmac.com/apple-history/get-a-mac-ad-campaign-ends) ran to 66 ads, and what made it work was not an attack. The PC was competent, formal, built for business. The Mac was personal and integrated. The message was simple: there is more than one kind of computer, because there is more than one kind of user.

The advisory market has the same split. AI is making it visible.

## What the big firms do well

Let us be fair from the start. The large consultancies are excellent organisations. They bring scale no boutique can match. A multinational that has to transform in forty countries at once, with compliance in every jurisdiction and change management in every business unit, needs a partner that can mobilise hundreds of people in a month. That is not a weakness of boutiques. It is a different assignment.

Big firms deliver strategy, roadmaps, board materials and, sometimes most importantly, institutional cover. Their research is world class and their name opens doors. For enterprise transformation, they are the right PC.

The founder of The Outlier spent nearly two decades inside large international organisations: fourteen years at ING Bank, then Heineken International and Media.Monks, across HR, Global Mobility, Governance, Compliance and Global Transformation. From that seat you see the big firms at their best. You also see where their model starts to strain.

### The pyramid has a cost

The classic consulting model is a pyramid. Partners sell. A layer of managers coordinates. A wide base of junior consultants does most of the analysis. That structure is efficient at scale, and it is the reason big firms can staff ten workstreams at once.

It also means the person analysing your firm is often early in their career, while the person with twenty years of judgement sits two layers away. Context leaks at every handover: partner to manager, manager to analyst, analyst to the implementation partner. Each step is professional. Each step loses a little of the why.

Personal attention is simply hard at scale. Nobody designed it out. The model just does not have much room for it.

## What a boutique does differently

A boutique firm runs a different operating system. The person who maps your friction is the person who designs the system, and the person accountable for whether it works. There is no handover from senior to junior. There is no context lost between workstreams.

That is the Mac: fewer programs, better integrated. Where a PC firm puts every step in a separate team, a boutique runs everything through the same hands. The pace changes as a result. Where an enterprise programme can spend months on mobilisation before anything is measured, a boutique can put a first measurement on the table in two weeks.

### Why this matters more for firms your size

Your firm probably has between 50 and 100 people. You are bigger than a small practice and nowhere near a corporation. That middle position shows up in the data. According to [Eurostat](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Use_of_artificial_intelligence_in_enterprises), 55% of large EU enterprises used AI technologies in 2025, against roughly 30% of medium-sized enterprises (50 to 249 employees) and 17% of small ones.

The gap is not about ambition. Eurostat itself points to the complexity of implementation, economies of scale and cost. Large organisations can afford an internal AI team. Firms of your size usually cannot, and should not try to build one just to keep up.

## AI moves the line

AI changes the economics of advice. The value of an advisor sits less and less in gathering and summarising information, and more and more in judgement: what does this mean for this firm, this client, this market? That is exactly the work that separates twenty years of experience from a template.

For large firms, AI is a productivity layer on a pyramid that will keep existing. For boutiques, AI is a multiplier on something that was already distinctive: senior attention per client.

There is a catch, and it applies to everyone. Adoption is wide, impact is narrow. The [McKinsey State of AI 2025 survey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai) found that 88% of organisations now use AI in at least one business function, yet only 39% report any impact on EBIT at enterprise level, and most of those say it is under 5%. Using AI is easy. Getting it to change how work actually gets done is the hard part.

### Where the Mac model has an edge

The same McKinsey research shows that the organisations getting real value are far more likely to have fundamentally redesigned their workflows. Redesigning a workflow is not a slide. It means sitting with the people who do the work, understanding where the time goes, and building something that fits into Monday morning.

That is where small, senior teams have a structural advantage. Fewer layers means fewer translations. The person who heard the problem from your consultants is the person who builds the fix. A concrete example of what that looks like in practice: instead of a forty-page report on "AI opportunities in professional services", you get a measurement of your three heaviest flows, such as proposals, client onboarding and file building, with hours lost per week and the system that would win them back.

## When you should choose the PC

This is a comparison, not a takedown. Choose a large firm when you need enterprise-wide transformation with governance across dozens of business units, or when the board needs the comfort of a global brand. Those are real needs, and the big firms serve them well.

Choose a boutique when your firm has 50 to 100 people, you can feel the friction in your processes, and you want a system built by the person who designed it. You will not get the brand. You will get the experience in the room, every time.

The Mac never sold by claiming the PC was bad. It sold by showing what it was made for.

## The comparison in one view

Here is the honest scorecard, row by row:

- **Scale and governance across many countries:** large firm. That is their craft.
- **Senior attention per client:** boutique, by design.
- **Speed from first measurement to working system:** boutique. Weeks rather than months.
- **Depth in one niche:** even, provided the boutique genuinely knows that niche.
- **Change management across forty business units:** only a large firm can do this.
- **Cost per working system delivered:** usually boutique, because a flat team carries less overhead than a pyramid.

None of these rows is a criticism. A firm choosing a partner is not choosing between good and bad. It is choosing between two operating systems, and the only question is which one fits your machine.
