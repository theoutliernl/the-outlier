Ask a managing partner where the firm loses time and you will usually hear the same answer: "Everywhere, a little." That answer is honest. It is also useless, because you cannot build a system for "everywhere".

In firms of 50 to 100 people, the time does not leak evenly. It pools in a small number of flows that repeat every week, touch many people, and were never redesigned as the firm grew. In advisory, mobility, HR, legal and recruitment firms, three of those flows come up again and again: proposals, client onboarding, and knowledge and documentation.

This article looks at each one, what the friction looks like, and what a realistic AI system for it looks like. Not a vision. A system your people would actually open on a Tuesday.

## Why these three flows

The pattern is not new. Back in 2012, the [McKinsey Global Institute](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/technology-media-and-telecommunications/our-insights/the-social-economy) estimated that the average interaction worker spent about 28% of the working week managing email and nearly 20% looking for internal information or tracking down colleagues who could help. More than a decade later, the problem has not gone away. [Microsoft's Work Trend Index](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/breaking-down-infinite-workday) found in 2025 that employees using Microsoft 365 are interrupted every two minutes during core hours by a meeting, email or chat.

The opportunity is real too. McKinsey's research on [the economic potential of generative AI](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/tech-and-ai/our-insights/the-economic-potential-of-generative-ai-the-next-productivity-frontier) concluded that current generative AI and other technologies have the potential to automate work activities that absorb 60 to 70% of employees' time today. Potential is not the same as practice, and that distinction matters. But it tells you where to look: language-heavy, repetitive work. In a boutique firm, that means proposals, onboarding and documentation.

There is one more reason to start here. The [MIT NANDA report on the GenAI Divide](https://mlq.ai/media/quarterly_decks/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf) noted that most AI budgets go to sales and marketing, while back-office automation often yields better returns. These three flows are exactly that kind of unglamorous, high-return work.

## Flow one: proposals

### What the friction looks like

Every proposal starts from a blank page, or from last month's proposal for a different client, with the wrong name still in paragraph four. Yet most of the content recurs: the firm's approach, scope language, team descriptions, fee structures, terms. The partner who won the conversation writes the proposal in the evening because nobody else knows exactly what was promised.

The cost is not only hours. It is turnaround time. A proposal that arrives in five days instead of two can lose a client who was ready to sign.

### What a realistic system looks like

A realistic proposal system does three things.

1. **It drafts from your own material.** It searches your past proposals and fills in the recurring sections in your firm's own phrasing, not generic text.
2. **It works from the call notes.** The partner's notes or a short structured intake form become the client-specific parts: the problem, the scope, the timeline.
3. **A partner approves the final version.** Always. The system prepares, the expert decides.

It lives in the tools where proposals are already written, usually Word in Microsoft 365 or Docs in Google Workspace. No new platform to log into.

### What to expect

Here is an illustration, not a client result. Suppose a proposal takes six hours and most of the content recurs. If a system handles the recurring parts and the partner spends their time on the parts that need judgement, the effort per proposal drops substantially. Multiply that by the number of proposals per week and the hours become visible. The point of a scan is to replace this illustration with your own numbers.

## Flow two: client onboarding

### What the friction looks like

A new engagement is good news. It also triggers the same manual routine every time: pulling a contract from templates, running compliance and identity checks by hand, creating the client file in two or three systems, writing the welcome email, chasing missing documents.

In mobility and HR firms the routine is often heavier, because each assignee or candidate brings their own documents, deadlines and country rules. Every step is small. Together they eat a significant share of someone's week, and errors at this stage follow the client for months.

### What a realistic system looks like

A realistic onboarding system is less about clever AI and more about a well-designed flow with AI in the right places.

- **One intake, many outputs.** Client details are entered once and flow into the contract template, the file structure and the CRM.
- **A checklist that runs itself.** Compliance steps are tracked automatically, with a person signing off on each check that requires judgement.
- **Drafted, not sent.** Welcome emails and document requests are drafted in the firm's voice, and a person reviews before anything leaves the building.
- **Missing items surface early.** The system flags what is still outstanding instead of someone discovering it a week later.

This is also where governance matters most. Onboarding handles personal data, sometimes sensitive data. The system should run in your existing, approved environment, with clear rules on what data goes where.

### What to expect

Onboarding systems usually take longer to build than a proposal system, because they connect to the file system and the CRM. That is why, in a typical roadmap, onboarding comes second: once the firm has seen one system work and trusts the approach.

## Flow three: knowledge and documentation

### What the friction looks like

This flow is the hardest to see, because it hides inside everything else. A consultant needs the latest status on a file and searches the mailbox, the document system and a spreadsheet. A client asks a simple question and the answer takes twenty minutes to assemble. A senior colleague answers the same internal question for the fifth time this month.

The firm's knowledge exists. It is just scattered across inboxes, folders and people's heads. When one of those people leaves, part of it leaves too.

### What a realistic system looks like

A realistic knowledge system is an internal assistant that answers questions in plain language, based on the firm's own files and nothing else.

- **It cites its sources.** Every answer points to the document it came from, so the consultant can check it in one click.
- **It respects permissions.** People only see answers drawn from files they already have access to.
- **It says when it does not know.** A good assistant admits a gap instead of filling it with something plausible.

### What to expect

The knowledge assistant is often the most valuable system in the long run, and the one that most depends on clean data. That is why it usually comes third. The proposal and onboarding systems tidy up the firm's material along the way, and the assistant benefits from that order.

## What a realistic roadmap looks like

Put together, a realistic plan for a firm of 70 people does not try to do all three at once. It looks more like this:

- **Now:** the proposal system. Largest direct time saving, lowest risk, visible to the whole partnership.
- **Next quarter:** onboarding. It needs integration with the file system, so it follows once the first system has proved itself.
- **After that:** the knowledge assistant, built on the cleaner data the first two systems produce.
- **Not now:** anything nobody is asking for. A client portal, for example, is often on the wish list and rarely on the list of things clients actually request.

There are also steps a firm can take on its own, without any outside help. Name one partner as owner of systems, a single name rather than a committee. Collect the last two years of proposals in one folder. Retire the spreadsheet that nobody maintains.
