# The Outlier vs the large IT integrators: Accenture, Capgemini and others

When a company wants AI built rather than just recommended, the large IT integrators are often the first names that come to mind. Accenture and Capgemini are the best known in Europe, and firms like them run some of the largest technology programmes in the world.

If you lead a boutique firm of 50 to 100 people, the question is not whether these companies are capable. They clearly are. The question is whether their model fits a partnership that needs one or two well-built systems, not a multi-year transformation programme.

This article sets out the differences honestly. Think of the old Mac and PC adverts. The PC was the dependable workhorse of the enterprise, built to run thousands of desks the same way. The Mac was built for people who wanted something that simply worked for them. Both have their place. We are the Mac.

These are genuine strengths. The integrators have engineering depth, partnerships with every major cloud and software vendor, delivery centres that can run around the clock, and the discipline to deliver very large programmes. When a global insurer needs to migrate core systems, connect hundreds of applications and run AI on top of all of it, that capacity is hard to match.

## Where the model and the boutique firm diverge

Integrators are built to deliver technology at volume. That shapes how they work, and it is not a flaw. It is what their clients need.

For a 50 to 100 person partnership, the fit can be awkward in a few places. These are general patterns in the industry and our own opinion, not claims about any specific project:

- **Programme thinking.** Delivery methods designed for large enterprises come with governance layers, steering committees and formal change requests. Useful at scale. Heavy when your whole firm fits in one office.
- **Platform gravity.** Large integrators often work through strategic alliances with major software vendors. That can mean new platforms and licences, when a small firm may get more value from the tools it already pays for.
- **Leverage and delivery centres.** Like most large professional services firms, integrators typically combine senior architects with larger delivery teams, often across several locations. Harvard Business Review described this leveraged pyramid model, and how AI is starting to reshape it, in 2025 ([HBR](https://hbr.org/2025/09/ai-is-changing-the-structure-of-consulting-firms)).
- **Technology first, process second.** A builder naturally starts from what to build. In a partnership, the harder question is often how partners, associates and support staff actually work, and where the time leaks away.

## When an integrator is the right choice

We want to be clear about this. A large integrator is the right choice if you need to replace or connect core systems across many entities. It is the right choice if you need 24/7 managed services and support across time zones. It is the right choice if you are building custom software that needs a large engineering team, or if your AI work depends on a deep integration with an enterprise platform where the integrator is a certified partner.

If that is your situation, their scale is an asset, not overhead.
